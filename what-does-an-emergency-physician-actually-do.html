What Does an Emergency Physician Actually Do?

Besides drink cold coffee.

November 16, 2026 3 min read Dr. Eric Cummins, MD
← Back to ED Insights

When I tell people at a party that I'm an emergency physician, one of 2 things happens.

They ask about the craziest thing I've ever seen.

Or they roll up a sleeve and show me a rash.

(For the record: I don't know what the rash is. Not at a party. Not without better lighting.)

Fair questions, though. Most people have no idea what we do until the day they need us.

So here's the short version.

Our specialty is "not sure yet"

A cardiologist knows hearts. A neurologist knows brains.

We know emergencies.

You don't arrive with a diagnosis. You arrive with a problem. Chest pain. A terrible headache. A daughter who blacked out. A grandfather who took too many of his pills and is now acting strange.

Our job is to figure out what's dangerous. Fast.

Most doctors are trained to think of the likely thing first. We're trained to think of the worst thing first. Then we try to prove it wrong.

It makes us useful in a crisis. It makes us terrible at watching medical dramas.

The part nobody sees

A while back, a 50-year-old man came in with a heart attack so severe that his heart stopped.

No pulse. Not breathing.

Our team started chest compressions. I placed a breathing tube. We shocked his heart. We gave the medications that keep blood moving.

His heart started again.

Then the cardiologist took him to open the blocked artery. That's the part that fixes the problem.

But he had to be alive to get there.

That's the job. We get you to the next step.

We don't always have the answer

I'd rather tell you that now.

Sometimes you leave the ED without a name for what's wrong. That's frustrating. I know.

What we can usually tell you is what it isn't. Not a heart attack. Not a stroke. Not the thing that can't wait until Monday.

If we have the answer, you'll get it. If we don't, we'll say so. Then we'll help you figure out what comes next.

Come see us for these

Call 911 or come to the emergency department for:

  • Chest pain
  • Severe trouble breathing
  • A drooping face, or weakness in an arm or leg
  • Passing out
  • A seizure
  • Bleeding that won't stop
  • A serious head injury
  • A severe allergic reaction
  • Poisoning, or too much of a medication
  • Coughing up or vomiting blood
  • A badly broken bone
  • Thoughts of suicide. You can also call or text 988.

Urgent care is great for the smaller stuff. Earaches. Rashes. Minor cuts. A fever that just won't quit.

It isn't a substitute for the ED. If you show up there with something serious, they'll send you to us. That costs time you may not have.

Not sure which one you need? I wrote a whole guide for that.

Anyone. Anything. Anytime.

We don't turn anyone away. The law says so. So does our conscience.

Insured or not. Rich or not. Famous or not. We don't check.

And we don't close.

Christmas. Rosh Hashanah. Thanksgiving. Diwali. 3 AM on a Tuesday.

Somebody's here. The lights are on. The coffee is terrible.

I hope you never need me. Nothing personal.

But if you do, we're ready.

— Dr. Eric Cummins, MD

This article is for general education only and is not a substitute for medical advice. If you think you are having an emergency, call 911 or go to the nearest emergency department.
